Answer given (content marker) — synthesises [[PSMA PET-CT]] + [[PET (Positron Emission Tomography)]]:

What it is: a molecular imaging scan that fuses a PSMA-targeted radiotracer (the "what to look for") + PET detection (the "how we see it") + CT (the anatomical map).

The scan step by step:
1. Inject a radiolabeled PSMA ligand — a small molecule that binds PSMA, tagged with a positron-emitter (⁶⁸Ga-PSMA-11, or ¹⁸F-DCFPyL / ¹⁸F-PSMA-1007).
2. Uptake wait (~60 min): the ligand circulates and binds PSMA on the surface of prostate-cancer cells; unbound tracer clears.
3. PET detection: the isotope emits positrons → annihilation → two 511 keV photons 180° apart → coincidence detection → 3-D map of where PSMA is concentrated.
4. CT at the same time gives anatomy; images are fused. Uptake quantified as SUV.
Result: PSMA-expressing prostate cancer "lights up" — primary, lymph nodes, bone, visceral mets.

Why it's used:
- Staging higher-risk prostate cancer.
- Localizing biochemical recurrence (rising PSA after treatment) at far lower PSA than conventional imaging.
- Much more sensitive than CT + technetium bone scan (smaller/earlier lesions).
- Theranostics: same PSMA target selects patients for ¹⁷⁷Lu-PSMA radioligand therapy; the imaging confirms the tumour expresses the target you're about to irradiate.

Caveats:
- PSMA is not 100% prostate-specific — physiologic uptake in salivary/lacrimal glands, kidneys, liver, bowel, and some benign or other tumours → potential false positives; needs expert read.
- Not all prostate cancer is PSMA-positive (PSMA-low/negative disease exists) → a negative scan doesn't fully exclude.
- Response-assessment criteria still evolving (RECIP); excellent for detection/selection, not yet the standard response criterion (rPFS still uses conventional RECIST/PCWG3).
